Can we be more cost effective?

Can a cheaper model do it?

Each card compares two ways to do the same job. Predict which costs less per finished task.

    Real coding tasks

    A frontier model: 92.3% solved, $1.19 per solved task
    A mid-tier model: 92.8% solved, $0.22 per solved task

    Same quality, about a fifth of the cost.

    Same model family, newer version

    Older version: 100% of the cost per solved task (baseline)
    Newer version: 13% of that cost, and about 10 points more tasks solved

    The upgrade paid for itself: better and about 87% cheaper per solved task.

    Hard science questions

    A lightweight model: 85% correct, about a twentieth of the cost per correct answer
    A mid-tier model: 91% correct, about 20 times the cost per correct answer

    A small drop in quality for a very large saving. Is it worth it? That depends on the job.

    Coding tasks

    Strong model on everything: 95.3% solved, $0.29 per task
    Cheap first, strong on the failures: about 97% solved, about $0.17 per task

    Routing beats brute force.

    Deep research, two effort settings

    Low effort: 66% rubric score at $4.48 per task
    High effort: 65% rubric score at $6.89 per task

    Trying harder didn’t help here. It just cost more.

    The same model, the same job

    A detailed memo: $1.40 per task
    One structured line: $0.49 per task, equally accurate

    The format you ask for drives the bill.

    The cheapest model isn’t always the cheapest way to get the job done. Test it on your tasks.

    Engineering tactics for cost reduction

    Knowing where these fit takes someone who knows how data moves through your business.

    The support desk

    For the next few minutes, you’re running Brew Diligence’s customer support desk.

    Half the room will follow a flowchart exactly. The other half will follow a prompt and use their judgment.

    Work in small groups. Designate one person to have your flowchart or prompt ready to go. Everyone else works through the customer messages and decides what to do.

    The prompt

    You answer customers for Brew Diligence, a coffee roaster.

    Keep good customers happy and be fair to the business.

    Refunds under $30 are fine if the story is believable.

    Send anything about safety, legal threats, or a long-time customer who is upset to a person.

    Don’t refund the same customer twice in 90 days.

      Some ways to mix rules and models

      • Fixed rules decide.Like the flowchart team.
      • A model decides.Like the prompt team.
      • A model sorts, and people take the hard ones.Like “send to a person” done well.

      For your own problem: how good does the judgment need to be, and what mistakes would you accept in exchange for speed?

      Use models from more than one lab: to compare them, and so they check each other’s work.
